The distilled system of *Framed Ink: Drawing and Composition for Visual Storytellers* by Marcos Mateu-Mestre (Design Studio Press) — the user's locked reference for how shots are staged, framed, lit for story, and strung together. Consult it on **every image and video prompt**. It is the PRIORITY source for composition and staging: when this file and generic "cinematic" habits disagree, this file wins.

Two boundaries, same as the Visual Quality Codex:

- **Knowledge, not structure.** This file changes WHAT a prompt specifies — never how a prompt is built, named, versioned, or delivered. Deck format, research gates, and module templates stay untouched.
- **Style-neutral.** The book teaches in black-and-white ink, but the system is value logic, staging logic, and camera psychology — it runs identically under a painted Fortiche frame, a photoreal render, a flat 2D cartoon, or a product shot. B&W examples never mean the output should be B&W.

Relationship to `visual-quality.md`: where both speak (value structure, silhouette, depth planes) they agree. **Framed Ink is the deeper authority on staging, camera, lighting-for-story, and shot-to-shot continuity; the Visual Quality Codex stays the authority on rendering craft** (materials, colour systems, edge control). Use both on every job.

Relationship to rule 9 (kinetic default): Framed Ink governs what each shot SAYS and how shots RELATE — never the cut rate. Do not import a calm-camera default from it; its quiet tools are for quiet story beats. Ramping, contrast-between-cuts, and geography rules below actively serve fast cutting.

**How to apply it: by shot type, fitted to the project.** Read §0 first — it governs everything below. A dialogue two-shot pulls different rules than an action frame or a product hero. Run §1 (pre-flight) and §2 (two-thumbnail method) on everything; then jump to the §4 Playbook entry for the job; pull devices from §3 as needed; for VIDEO/SHOTLIST add §5; check §7 on the second pass.

## 0. Fit the project first — the senior-artist rule

This codex is a palette, not a checklist. A real artist carries knowledge across every platform and style — and knows exactly which piece serves which job. Pick the devices THIS shot and THIS project need; forcing the full system into every prompt damages it as surely as ignoring it.

**What always runs (every project, every style):** the composition skeleton — §1 purpose and intensity, §2 two-thumbnail thinking, §3's staging devices, §4 shot-type staging, §5 continuity. Framing, staging, camera psychology, eye control, and shot-to-shot logic are style-agnostic physics of attention: they hold identically in a noir frame, a pastel flat-2D cartoon, a bright beauty commercial, and a painted battle scene.

**What adapts to the project (the light MOOD):** §3.3's mechanics are universal — light aims the eye, contrast peaks at the story point, exposure level sets unease, light politics rank characters, day/night is surface logic. But the book's chiaroscuro FLAVOR — heavy black masses, pools carved from darkness, concealed sources, crop-by-darkness — is a mood palette, opt-in by project. On bright, flat, high-key, pastel, sunny, or brand-locked looks, express the same mechanics through THAT look's own language: "highest contrast at the story point" might be a saturation accent, a cleaner edge, a warmer patch on a cool field — not a black void. Never force noir lighting onto a project whose look says otherwise; never abandon the light-as-attention mechanics either.

**Under an art direction (attached decks, brand guidelines, locked styles like GRUESOME / kok-boru):** the art direction rules the SURFACE — palette, lighting mood, render register, stylization, every locked element. This codex rules the SKELETON underneath any surface. Follow the direction's own composition/lighting guidance where it speaks; where it is silent, or where deeper craft strengthens its intent, bring Framed Ink without asking permission — an art director hands a deck to an artist expecting them to add the craft the deck doesn't spell out. Never contradict a locked element (SKILL.md DOCUMENT rules), and never shrink the prompt to only what the document says: support, extend, and elevate the direction — don't hide behind it, don't fight it.

## 1. Pre-flight — the shot exists for a reason (feeds rule 2a INTENT FIRST)

Mateu-Mestre's first move is never visual. Before composing, answer in one line each:

1. What is the **whole story** about — and what is **this specific shot** about within it?
2. What **mood** should the audience be in at exactly this moment?
3. What is the **function** of this moment — setup, escalation, breather, reveal, climax?
4. What in the frame **contributes** to that statement?
5. What can be **left out** without changing what the shot says?

**The narrowing-down ladder.** Settle the shot top-down, in this order: (a) the general **level and type of intensity** the image must carry for its place in the story — extreme joy? subdued dread? coiled suspense?; (b) the **narrative specifics** — who does exactly what, where, with what; (c) the **artistic execution and style** that delivers it. Intensity first, facts second, finish third. A prompt that starts at "style" with no decided intensity produces pretty noise.

The image is the vehicle, not the end. A gorgeous frame that doesn't serve its story moment is worthless in context — and a shot must work twice: on its own, AND between the shots before and after it.

**Selective vision (the squint).** We don't see inventories; we see what matters to us at the moment. Squint at the imagined scene: only what survives the squint visually exists. Describe what exists **under this light**, not everything physically present — when every feature is equally important, no choice was made and the frame says nothing. Frame and illuminate to show **what** needs showing, **how** it needs showing — and crop out the rest (by framing or by darkness). Spirit over transcription: a "photographic" depiction is not the answer; expression, transmitted energy, and mood are.

Make the audience **feel** things, don't tell them things — insinuate and foreshadow rather than telegraph; use simplicity, shadows, and silences instead of an avalanche of detail; always leave them wondering about a little something. Consistency is part of this: the first shots of a project establish the "rules of the game" — tone, drama level, visual code. Every later prompt in the same project honors that world or breaks it deliberately as a story event.

## 2. The two-thumbnail method — design the frame before you dress it

The book's signature move. Every frame is designed as two abstract statements BEFORE any "elements" (characters, furniture, rocks) exist. Think in basic, clear, readable shapes first — then compose the elements within them.

**Thumbnail 1 — the light/dark mass statement.** Reduce the frame to 2–3 big value masses that ARE the emotion. Oppression = heavy dark masses squeezing one small light gap where the character stands ("visually strangle" him). Freedom = one small dark figure on a vast light field. Menace = one hard bright shape cutting a black frame. If the mass statement doesn't carry the mood on its own, no amount of detail will — and a frame built on simple masses stays a fast, clear read no matter how complex the acting and detail on top get.

**Thumbnail 2 — the lines of tension.** The frame's directional skeleton: physical lines (a road, a rifle, a fence, a shadow edge, even the texture grain of rocks and terrain) plus implied lines from "connecting the dots" (three heads forming an arc; a pistol butt → the thug's knuckles → the victim's head). Every strong line should converge on the story point; kill any line that drags the eye out of frame or to nothing.

→ *Prompt move:* write BOTH statements into the prompt in plain language — e.g. "frame reads as three masses: black canyon walls left and right crushing a narrow bright gap, the lone rider a dark cut-out inside the light; rock striations and rope lines all converge on him." Name the masses, name what converges.
→ *Test:* shrink it mentally to a thumbnail — do 2–3 masses still read, and is the story point where the lines meet and the contrast peaks?

## 3. The device toolkit — psychology → prompt phrase

Each device: what it does emotionally → how to spend words on it. These are the concrete vocabulary rule 2b wants instead of dead adjectives.

**3.1 Camera height & power staging.**
- **Below the subject's eyes (upshot):** towering, imposing, in control, epic. Works on anything — a warrior, a city canyon; taken to the extreme the vanishing point enters the frame overhead. A subject looking out of frame, opposite everyone else, reads independent.
- **Above the subject (downshot):** diminished, trapped, in trouble; the environment dominates and we barely see what surrounds them — exactly how they feel. Reinforce: other characters' eyes higher in frame than the subject's; subject's body below the horizon line; surrounding shapes (leaves, fists, hats, a shadow over the head) aiming at them to "close the circle" — no way out.
- **Eye level:** neutral, human, documentary honesty. A straight horizon = stability and calm; a tilted (dutch) frame = unease, something wrong.
- **The dominance stack (power beyond height).** The dominant figure: sits higher in frame with **no head above his**; head above the horizon line while the others sit below it; **watching while unwatched**; armed; **detached** from the group whose silhouettes overlap into one mass; the **only one in motion** — and uphill motion at that (nothing he does is effortless = greatness); more ornate kit than the foot soldiers. Stack several markers for authority; invert them one by one to dethrone a character.
→ *Prompt move:* name the height AND its purpose — "camera low, below his jawline, so he towers against the ceiling beams; his head the highest in frame, the others' silhouettes merged below the horizon."

**3.2 Lens psychology (distance is meaning, not zoom).**
- **Wide-angle, camera close:** depth cues exaggerated to the point of distortion — near things balloon, features stretch (nose vs. cheekbones vs. ears), perspective slams; outrageously dynamic, aggressive, unstable. A one-point-perspective downshot with a wide lens adds vertigo and motion feel.
- **~50mm:** how the human eye sees; no distortion; sincerity.
- **Long lens, camera far:** planes compress and flatten; subject and background appear stacked close together; depth cues minimized; realistic proportions kept; feels observed, isolated, fated — the world looms flat behind the subject.
- **Background reach (same subject size, different worlds).** With the face the same size in frame: wide lens close = the face big AND the whole environment wraps distorted behind it (background = a world); long lens far = only a sliver of background remains beside the face (background = a detail). Lens choice decides **how much world accompanies the subject**.
→ *Prompt move:* state lens + camera distance + what it does — "wide lens inches from the wheels so the near wheel dwarfs the ones a few yards back" / "long lens from across the valley, the pines stacked flat behind her, barely a door's width of the house visible behind her head."

**3.3 Lighting for story (the perception engine).** Lighting is the first answer to "how do we make one location tell two different stories?" — by changing the light we radically change the perceived reality. **Two layers here (per §0): the MECHANICS below are universal; the chiaroscuro DRESSING (black masses, carved pools, crop-by-darkness) is a mood palette — translate the mechanics into the project's own light language when its look is bright, flat, or art-directed.** The full device set:

- **a) Light IS reality.** What light doesn't touch doesn't visually exist. All lights on, windows open = everything seen, every detail known = confident, relaxed, nothing left to imagination. Lights off = objects stop existing visually; the unknown does the acting. Same room: a children's tale or the most upsetting horror film, on lighting alone.
- **b) Light as pointer.** The eye lands on the highest tonal contrast: put the darkest dark against the lightest light AT the story point and nowhere else. A bright zone framed by darkness is a spotlight the audience cannot refuse; a point of major contrast = visual tension exactly where needed. In a busy organic frame, give ONE hottest spot for the fast read (and if everything else is foliage-chaos, let the hottest spot also hold the frame's only straight line — the eye snaps to it).
- **c) The exposure ladder (faces and anything else).** Mood = distance from the everyday impression: fully lit and relaxed → half in shadow → slashed by blind-shadows → features barely surfacing from black = nightmare. Decide how much is revealed and how clearly — that dial travels from friendly to unsettling.
- **d) Crop by light.** Lighting crops the frame as surely as the camera does: leave out by darkness what you'd otherwise leave out by framing. A small lit fragment pushed to an extreme position in a black field reads unnatural and unpredictable — a foreboding device.
- **e) The concealed source.** Hide the light source behind a big foreground mass so only the lit SURFACES read (the faces in a midnight room). Whoever shows their eyes and full expression becomes the center of the action — especially when the wall lines also point at them and the others watch them.
- **f) Day vs. night is surface logic, not sky.** The sky can hold the same value in both. Night = flatten everything to black graphic shapes and let one or two pools/reflections carve out only what matters. Day = a majority of lit surfaces PLUS reflected light inside the shadows — "light is the predominant element." What matters is the light source and **how it reflects on the elements**; prioritize the reflections.
- **g) Shadow as actor.** A shadow cast over the subject's head closes the trap. Cast shadows point. A properly cast shadow implies everything you didn't draw — one lit hussar plus the right shadows = the whole regiment (insinuation + one-vs-many). Backlight = confusion, disorientation, mystery (a backlit POV says "I just woke up and don't understand"); rim-lit anonymous shapes = menace.
- **h) Two-character light politics.** Both equally lit = equal footing, fluent exchange. One lit, one shadowed = intent asymmetry (the lit one reads better-intentioned). Whoever carries the bigger tonal contrast owns the viewer's eye — assign the contrast deliberately (see Playbook C).
- **i) Light in motion.** Design lighting accents INTO a move: clouds parting and light arriving as she turns = a designed emotional transition, not an accident. A single camera journey can pass through distinct light zones (sunny loud street → private sunlit garden → dim interior → subtle complex hall) so the emotional temperature shifts en route without a cut. Whatever freezes mid-move must still obey (b).
→ *Prompt move:* think value-first and reflection-first — "shadow-dominant frame, single hot pool on the table between them, his face half-carved by it, hers fully lit; the window behind holds the frame's only straight lines" (this replaces "dramatic lighting").

**3.4 Line & shape language.** Horizontals = calm, stability, rest. Verticals = assertive, rigid, authority. Diagonals = dynamic, aggressive, unstable, alarm. Curves = subtle, peaceful, organic, kinder to the eye. Jagged/angular = threat, harshness, conflict; spiky X-silhouettes read action even in a still, rounder steadier masses read calm even mid-action. Lines can be anything — a branch, graffiti, a row of clouds, a cast shadow, the imaginary line linking heads — and they steer the eye to a chosen element. When the background's perspective lines run the same direction as the action, the action reads stronger. Opposing line families inside one shot (spears' diagonal vs. clouds' diagonal) counteract each other into balance; opposing shape languages between two characters or two worlds (curved home vs. angular home) codes personalities and foreshadows conflict the moment they meet.
→ *Prompt move:* give the frame ONE dominant line family that matches the beat, and say what the lines point at.

**3.5 Screen direction.** For left-to-right readers, movement L→R feels positive, progressing; R→L feels like struggle, going against the grain. Keep one direction across a sequence for momentum and clarity; reverse it only as a deliberate dramatic turn.

**3.6 Look direction & lead room.** The audience looks where a prominent character looks, expecting story there — so aim gazes at the story point, and leave space in the frame ahead of a look or a movement. A downward gaze-line from one character onto others = visual dominance and control over them; a weapon held near the chest, pointed their way, adds silent pressure along the same line.

**3.7 Negative space as an active actor.** Emptiness presses or liberates. A character small under a huge dark mass overhead = crushed, cornered. Small against a vast open field = free, or utterly alone — the light decides which. The space between two people is their relationship (see Playbook C). Backs of heads and shoulders in the near foreground can turn negative space into a frame around the object of desire — the audience peers with the character.

**3.8 Size difference / big–medium–small.** Even masses are static; uneven masses (one big, one medium, one small) make a frame deeper, more dynamic, more interesting. Importance = size: make the key element actually bigger or place it closer to camera — and this weight relationship can shift across the story as relevance shifts. An element allowed to break the frame ("so big it doesn't fit") reads overwhelming — monsters, machines, authority. Scale the environment to its inhabitants to sell a giant: a door barely big enough for him to squeeze through, every object in the room drawn to the building's size, and him not fitting the shot.

**3.9 One vs. many ("tree vs. forest").** One element = a character with a story; many similar elements = a mass, a texture, a force. Show only one or a few elements and cast the right shadows, and the audience imagines the complex whole — insinuation scales better than inventory, and the uncertainty adds mystery or dread on demand.

**3.10 Order vs. chaos.** Rigid geometric repetition (a grid of identical windows) = cold, institutional, overpowering — a well-structured maze to get lost in. Organic, irregular placement = improvised, disorganized, humanized, alive. Materials carry it too: uneven brick and peeling paint humanize; sheer metal, glass, and concrete institutionalize. If the space was chosen by a character, the space's adjectives become the character's — a full personality established by architecture.

**3.11 Complex vs. simple.** Ornate density and bare simplicity are two worlds; stories are passages of characters from one state to another, and crossing between visual worlds IS that passage made visible. One character can belong to both worlds at different moments of their arc. In a busy frame the simplest zone pops; in a sparse frame the one complex element pops — and vice versa: the one twisted magical tree among straight trunks, or the one perfectly straight tree in a twisted forest, is unmistakably the story's turning point.

**3.12 Conceal & reveal.** What's hidden powers emotion. Obscure the partner's expression and the audience shares the protagonist's question; show the reaction before the cause; keep the mystery by lighting only what the story needs; a low beam from a flashlight outside the frame both hides and reveals. "Less is more" is an emotional tool, not a budget.

**3.13 Weird beats, weird shots.** Match compositional strangeness to story strangeness: perfect symmetry where no epic function calls for it, elements that together seem out of context, extreme crops (a face pushed to the very edge of frame) — all foreshadow, unsettle, and build anticipation. Often achieved simply by cropping OUT the elements that would make the scene look natural. Earn it — a weird frame on a normal beat just confuses.

**3.14 Tangents & coincidences.** Elements that just touch, heads grazing the horizon, twin sizes and equal spacing — accidents that read as intended and yank the audience out of the story ("what looks weird" steals the read). Overlap decisively or separate decisively; vary sizes and gaps.

**3.15 Symmetry & thirds.** Perfect symmetry is reserved for epic, ritual, unquestionable-code moments (it announces "the rules here are absolute"); used casually it feels self-conscious and convenient. Default to off-center placement by thirds, main elements on the axes/intersections, never crowding the frame edges.

**3.16 Icons beyond faces.** Faces, eyes, hands are the default read points. To make the audience read something else first — a briefcase, a bottle, a gun, a product — iconize it: isolate it, light it, scale it, aim lines at it, and strip competing read points (crop faces out entirely when the object must carry the beat — concentrating on the agreed signal item makes US the spy looking for it). Shooting around the action often explains a moment better than the action: the chaos of spent shells and the camera pressed close to a soldier's boots carries the electric tension better than the firefight itself.

**3.17 Shot-size ladder.** Wide/long = context: where are we, what's in play, what will we interact with. Medium = still situational but interference cropped out. Close-up = the reaction now matters more than its cause. Extreme close-up = the audience becomes the character. POV = we're inside their head, seeing exactly what they see — the most direct way to put the audience in the movie; dress the POV with their state (backlit = confusion; tilted against the cops' "vertical stability" = disorientation; buried behind foliage layers = hidden, voyeuristic, afraid to be found). Cutting in (same axis, camera just moves forward) needs the subject to hold the same proportional position across both frames.

## 4. Playbook — route by what's being made

Run §1 + §2 always; then apply the entry that matches the job. Multiple entries can stack (an action frame inside an establishing wide, a product hero in a two-shot).

**A. Establishing / world shot.** The first image writes the rules of the game: geography, tone, era, code — all at once. Rigid symmetry + flat frontal staging = a world of unbreakable codes; deep perspective, rich textures, open smiling faces, era-true props and costume = a warm organic world. Choose horizon height (low horizon = monumental subject against sky; high = subject embedded in the terrain). Prompt carries: where we are, when we are, one dominant mood statement in the value masses, and 2–3 world-implying specifics.

**B. Single-character emotional shot.** Decide the power dial (3.1) and the exposure ladder (3.3c): how much of the face/figure is revealed and how clearly = how far the mood travels from everyday. Hair, costume, and their cast shadows are acting shapes. Negative space pressure (3.7) sets whether the world crushes or abandons them. One character + one environment already makes a two-hander: the room can be the antagonist.

**C. Dialogue / two-shot (the Restaurant rules).** The space between two people IS the relationship:
- Clear space between them + both equally lit = fluent, positive, balanced exchange.
- A visually heavy element between them = the dialogue is broken/obstructed; give them different backgrounds and they sit in "different worlds" while sharing a table.
- One lit, one in shadow = intent asymmetry (the lit one reads more honest).
- The one with stronger tonal contrast owns the frame — assign it deliberately.
- Eyeline height difference = power difference; camera behind the dominant one's shoulder makes us side with them; a downward gaze-line from one onto the other = control (3.6).
Establish the axis (the line) in the first prompt of the scene and stay on one side of it across every shot (§5.3).

**D. Three characters / groups.** Three heads form a shape — make it a deliberate triangle or arc that points where the story goes, never a flat lineup at equal spacing (equal = no hierarchy = no story). Rank the group by the dominance stack (3.1): size, contrast, elevation, motion, detachment. In crowds, use one-vs-many: silhouette-merge the group into one overlapping mass and detach the leader — highest head in frame, above the horizon line, the only one moving, more ornate kit.

**E. Action frame (single image or key moment).** Diagonals rule everything: subject, environment lines, camera tilt — same direction for momentum, opposed directions for impact/alarm. Get the camera INSIDE the action with a wide lens close (near limb/weapon huge, opponent small); let key elements break the frame; freeze the peak-intent instant or the aftermath, not the dead middle. Print motion into a still: fan repeated elements as phases of one movement (three guns held at successive angles = one swing, even the wall shadow following the fan), stagger multiple figures as successive positions of one action (a U-line through three parkour runners = one skateboarder's ramp path), one-point-perspective downshot + wide lens = vertigo. Chaotic small debris (shells, splinters, spray) at random angles carries the energy between the big shapes; the menacing weapon close to our face does the rest.

**F. Chase / speed / vehicle.** The ramp grammar (also §5.5): flat lateral profile = calm, neutral, "just passing"; introduce diagonals and uphill/downhill against a dark mass = interest turning menace; turn it toward camera in perspective = "coming at us"; long lens keeps it distant-safe even when huge and frame-breaking; end wide-lens-close, camera low and tilted so the ground plane feels unstable, near parts gigantic vs. parts a few yards back tiny = maximum aggression. Rhythmic elements streaming past (power poles, sleepers, rails) meter the speed behind the subject.

**G. Suspense / reveal / intercut logic.** Knowledge vs. unawareness: show the audience the threat the subject can't see, then cut to the oblivious subject — and manage the two tension levels explicitly: the initiative side bigger in their frame, their eyes higher, on the opposite screen side, so every cut reads as one visually chasing the other. Scale progression for reveals: establish size against a known reference, then re-anchor against a bigger one with a shared common element AND a physical contact/overlap point between the two frames (aliens beside humans → same aliens dwarfed at the ship's mouth). Conceal & reveal (3.12) and POV-withholding stretch the fuse; the payoff shot spends the contrast you saved. Showing the same element under different light across the story builds a perception progression — an arc the audience feels (§5.6).

**H. Landscape / environment as character.** Landscapes speak: verticals in a lit valley = reassuring depth; the same valley at night, shapes gone, the one light gap pushed far off-center = distress. Choose the land's shape rhythm for the beat (rounded = gentle, spiky = menace), decide day/night as a knowledge dial (3.3f), and either keep action out (eerie stillness), concentrate action where the light concentrates (fast read), or spring a surprise from the dark near-foreground.

**I. Product / object hero (icon logic, adapted).** Treat the object as the icon (3.16): isolate it in its own light pool or negative-space island, converge lines and gazes on it, give it the frame's peak contrast, and remove competing read points (hands and faces cropped or shadowed unless they serve the object). Big-medium-small still applies: hero big, context medium, detail cues small. This entry stacks with the Visual Quality Codex's opt-in studio/PBR block for realism jobs.

**J. Character design / model sheet / vinyl figure.** Silhouette first: the head and body must read as one distinct simple gray shape (blade, pear, bullet, block, flame) recognizable at any angle and size — design the silhouette before the features, and keep it consistent through the story. Body-shape families are personalities: smooth soft rounded masses = smoother, softer character; straight rigid verticals = upright, formal, unbending; broken spiky bulk = brutal, dangerous. Across a cast, every silhouette must differ in shape family, not just costume. Expression is shape language too: spiky star = aggressive; soft nested curves = appealing, insinuating; a long vertical reaching upward = someone straining above a reality they don't accept; mixing straights, curves, flats, and diagonals in one face = confusion (only if that IS the character); the hair mass amplifies whatever the face favors. If the character changes clothes across the story, lock one unmistakable silhouette feature (hairdo, head shape, headdress, big earrings, a tattoo, an accessory) that survives every outfit. Poses: one clear directional thrust through the whole body; expansive diagonals = release, action; a horizontal resting profile = relaxed, weight settled; shrinking vertically, curling, head sinking between shoulders = insecure, defeated; solid filled masses give a pose graphic power; profiles read walks and actions clearest.

**K. Comic page / manga page / multi-panel image / storyboard sheet.** The page is one composition seen all at once — design the page's own black/white balance and reading path before any panel's interior. Panel format is semantic: wide panoramic = scope, calm, time passing; square = neutral, static; tall vertical = height, falls, dominance, a claustrophobic slice; small repeated panels = staccato time ticks; a splash = the event; circles = peephole/lens/memory flavor; a jagged exploding border = impact and noise. Any shape is legal as long as it serves the narrative and never confuses the page's layout — the audience follows a story, they must never have to decode panel order; clear readability of the page outranks cleverness. Choreograph across panels: exits screen-right enter next panel screen-left; link a traveling object's positions into one smooth eye-curve across the page; keep "the line" between panels exactly as between film cuts; after two constrained panels, expand one horizontally to give the action room to run; a straight-on neutral face shot = the tense pause between actions. Fragment a scene into detail shards (eyes, hand, weapon, boots) to stretch time and build tension, then release wide; insets keep geography while cutting in. Save spectacular devices — overused, they leave nowhere to go when the truly intense moment comes; build rhythms, then break them for drama. Balloons and onomatopoeia are graphic shapes IN the composition, planned at rough-layout stage: they sit on the reading path, their tails choreograph speaking order, they can bridge or frame panels; sound words are drawn pictures of their sound — letterform size, weight, texture, and direction = the sound's volume, violence, and travel (a tiny repeated "plick" = a rhythm; a huge ragged roar = a mass with momentum).

## 5. Sequences — VIDEO cuts and SHOTLIST scenes (thinking in continuity)

For any multi-shot job (a packed VIDEO clip's internal cuts, or a whole SHOTLIST), every shot is composed AGAINST its neighbors. These rules feed rule 9's kinetic cutting — they make fast cuts readable, they never slow them down.

**5.1 Contrast between shots is the rhythm.** Contrast is why we see things at all — remove it and you get camouflage; the same law runs the story. An action beat hits harder sandwiched between two quiet ones; a close-up lands after a wide; a diagonal frame lands after a stable one; a character turns great as their opponent turns worse. Vary shot size, angle, direction, and value weight between consecutive prompts — never let two adjacent shots be near-twins, and when characters could be confused across a cut, flip the scene's main weight to the other side of the screen. Establish a rhythm precisely so the "special" shot can break it.

**5.2 Focal-point carry.** The screen is big and the audience must never hunt for the next focus — there is no time for it and no reason for it. Whenever possible, the last position of the focus of attention in one shot coincides with the first point of focus in the next. Place the story point of shot B where shot A left the eye — or leave a light/shape trail leading there. The screen itself is the one element all shots share: compose the flow ACROSS it as deliberately as within each frame. This is what makes hard cutting feel effortless.

**5.3 The line (180°).** Draw the axis between the scene's two main elements and keep every camera on ONE side: backgrounds stay consistent, each character keeps their screen side, geography stays free — the audience follows the story, not "where is what." Cross it only legally: a neutral on-axis shot (head-on/tail-on), a visible camera move across during the shot, or a cutaway that resets geography. The one sanctioned exception: break geography ON PURPOSE when the story wants the audience lost (waking up kidnapped somewhere unknown) — disorientation as a choice, never an accident. In a shotlist, note the axis once per scene and honor it in every prompt of that scene.

**5.4 Coverage: same spot vs. motivated moves.** A whole action can be covered from ONE camera position by letting the subject travel and re-cropping (wide → medium → close as they approach) — maximum geographic clarity, great economy, and even the crops read as different-feeling shots. Move the camera to a NEW position only when the move adds something: new information, new intimacy, a new emotional station. The line-of-fire dial makes it concrete: camera behind the shooter = we're with him (low intensity); camera moved near the line between him and his targets = unsettled (mid intensity); camera in front of the gun, wide lens = we ARE the target (maximum). A camera can also arc around the subject from downshot up to upshot in one move — a visual crescendo that turns a look into a revelation — while staying on one side of the line so screen sides hold. Every setup change must be a meaning change.

**5.5 Ramp to the climax.** Escalate frame energy across the sequence: flat/lateral/stable early → uneven shapes and diagonals → perspective convergence and closer crops → wide-lens-close distortion at the peak. Leave headroom: while the scene is still short of its climax, keep frames balanced (opposed diagonals counteracting each other) so there is somewhere left to go; save the wildest composition for the climax — spend it early and the top of the mountain is gone. After the peak, resolve: tension answered, frames settle, the audience exhales.

**5.6 Same elements, different message.** Re-staging is re-writing: the identical cast + props + location, re-framed and re-lit, tells a different story (safe distance = observation; engulfed by the house, wide lens, low camera = the house owns them now; tight on faces = we're with them; the bike kept small in the background = a geographic anchor through it all). In a shotlist this is the cheap way to evolve a scene — change the camera relationship, not the set. Extended across a story: showing the same element under different lights builds a progression in the audience's perception of it — an arc, and a ready-made transition device.

**5.7 Locked camera & depth staging.** With the camera still (or one continuous move), choreograph the scene in depth: characters grow/shrink in frame as power and attention shift; a foreground figure can be "part of the composition but not of the story" (face shadowed/hatted) until their moment; background faces all turned one way make the one exception THE story point of the lineup. Anchor trick: set the camera at the constant character's eye height — anyone whose eyes sit on the horizon line keeps that eye height at ANY distance from camera, so the frame stays anchored while they travel the set. Pre-plan so any character combination at any freeze moment composes cleanly — every possible still along the move must be a shot you'd sign.

**5.8 Approach in size beats & parallax.** A subject nearing (or a camera nearing it) reads in composed size beats — each beat its own frame with its own mass statement; growing overlap = growing threat; a camera low to the water/ground grants the arriving mass a position of superiority. Layer FG/MG/BG planes for lateral moves: wide lens close = violent parallax and drama; long lens far = planes glide together, stately. Keep ground-contact points accurate in low-camera shots — the tops of figures and the gaps between them must tell the same distance story as their feet, or things read gigantic (unless gigantic is the point).

**5.9 Transitions between scenes.** End a scene on an element that rhymes with the start of the next — a shape match, a tonal match, a direction match, or a shared motif — so the audience glides across the seam. In a shotlist, write the rhyme into the last prompt of scene N and the first of scene N+1.

**5.10 Mood travel inside one move.** A single camera journey through a set can pass through distinct lighting/mood zones (sunny loud street → private garden → dim dramatic interior → subtle complex hall) so the emotional temperature shifts en route without a cut — art-direct the zones along the path, and keep the characters' frame positions near-constant so the WORLD does the mood work.

## 6. Attention checklist — what the audience reads first

Ranked pull, for aiming or troubleshooting any frame: 1) the point of highest tonal contrast; 2) faces, then eyes, then hands; 3) where the biggest/nearest character is looking; 4) the vanishing point; 5) whatever the lines converge on; 6) the biggest mass; 7) the frame's one straight line if everything else is organic; 8) the highest-saturation zone (colour work — hand off to the VQ codex's colour system). If these pull to different places, the frame is arguing with itself — align them on the story point, or deliberately split them for a two-beat read (first read → second discovery).

## 7. Framed Ink pre-ship pass (runs with rule 5c's second pass)

- [ ] Shot purpose stated — you can say in one line what this frame is FOR, and its intensity level was chosen before its style?
- [ ] Two thumbnails present in words — 2–3 value masses named, and the lines of tension named, both converging on the story point?
- [ ] The squint survives — everything described exists under THIS light; the rest cropped out by frame or by darkness?
- [ ] Camera height + lens chosen for psychology (power dial, dominance stack, distance meaning, background reach) — not by habit?
- [ ] Project fit (§0) — art direction and locked style honored on the surface, Framed Ink supporting underneath; lighting MOOD matching the project's look, with noir dressing only where the look invites it?
- [ ] Lighting doing story work IN THE PROJECT'S OWN LIGHT LANGUAGE — source, reflections, exposure ladder, shadow-as-actor, contrast aimed at the story point — not just "dramatic lighting" and not forced chiaroscuro?
- [ ] Negative space doing a job (pressing, freeing, isolating) rather than left over?
- [ ] Sizes uneven (big–medium–small), no twins, no equal spacing, no tangents, main elements off the frame edges?
- [ ] Look direction, lead room, and screen direction serving the beat — and held across the sequence?
- [ ] Symmetry only if the moment is epic/ritual; frame-weirdness only if the beat is weird?
- [ ] Concealment considered — is there something the shot is stronger for NOT showing?
- [ ] Sequence jobs: contrast with neighbor shots, axis respected per scene with only legal crossings, focal point carried across cuts, headroom kept and the wildest frame saved for the climax, scene seams rhymed?

If a box the shot depends on is empty, fix the prompt before it ships.
