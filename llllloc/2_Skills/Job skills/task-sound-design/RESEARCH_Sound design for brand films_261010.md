# Fewer sounds, built in layers, ducked under the voice

The most effective method for one person doing sound design and mixing for brand films in Premiere Pro with Soundly is a fixed order of work that treats the voice-over as the anchor and every other sound as a decision, not a fill: lock the VO and the picture first, spot the film as a marker list of hero moments, lay a continuous ambience bed, build each designed event from **two or three layers (transient, body, sub)** pulled from Soundly rather than one stock whoosh, place and duck the music under the voice by **about 12-18 dB with 300-800 ms fades**, then mix to **-14 LUFS integrated at -1 dBTP** for web and social (a separate **-23 LUFS EBU R128** or **-24 LKFS ATSC A/85** export for broadcast TVCs), and deliver the full mix plus separate VO, music and SFX stems as 48 kHz 24-bit WAV. The craft principles that separate premium work from "cheap corporate" come straight from film, trailer and music-video sound: Walter Murch's finding that an audience follows only **two and a half elements at once**, Randy Thom's "if everything is loud, nothing is loud," the trailer recipe of a riser followed by near-silence and a single hit transient with a low-end drop, and the music-video rule that effects live only in a spectral gap and in time with the beat. The amateur tells are the mirror image of that method: an unmodified library whoosh on every cut, the same hit reused, music at a constant level under the VO, no high-pass filter, untrimmed tops and tails, no ambience bed and no silence. One honest caveat runs through the research: the level and ducking figures are practitioner rules of thumb, not standards, and Instagram and TikTok publish no loudness target at all, so the -14/-1 figure is the de facto convergence of 2025-2026 sources rather than a platform spec ([Fora Soft](https://www.forasoft.com/learn/audio-for-video/articles-audio/lufs-targets-per-platform-2026)).

## The order of work: voice first, music last, ducking after everything is locked

Every professional source agrees on where to start and where to end, and the order matters because Premiere's ducking tool destroys hand work if it is run too early. Motion designer Kyle Hemrick records the real VO before animating and locks "hero moments," the "meaningful sync ups between the visuals and the voice script," so nothing is animated twice ([Voices.com](https://www.voices.com/podcasts/voicebranding/?p=559)). Krotos lays out the pass order as sync and dialogue, then foley and ambience, then designed SFX and sweetening, then a quick mix and export, with music sitting "on top," and its Premiere guide starts with project prep: bins by category, renamed takes, and "markers for key frames, hits and edit points" ([Krotos, Film sound design](https://krotos.studio/blog/film-sound-design); [Krotos, Premiere](https://krotos.studio/blog/sound-design-premiere)). Pro Sound Effects adds the one rule that reconciles the "music before or after SFX" question: never design "in a vacuum," keep music and dialogue in the session while building effects ([Pro Sound Effects](https://blog.prosoundeffects.com/sound-design-mistakes-you-should-never-make)). So the temp music goes in early, and the final music level and ducking are set last.

The reconciled one-person sequence is therefore: clean and tightly trim the VO on A1 (Jonny Elwyn's advice is to trim VO regions tightly to remove breaths and mouth noise) and lock picture to it; drop temp music in so SFX are judged in context; spot the sequence with markers; lay the ambience bed; build designed SFX on three layered tracks; place final music; run ducking; mix, then check on phone and in mono; export the full mix and stems ([Jonny Elwyn](https://jonnyelwyn.co.uk/film-and-video-editing/sound-design-tips-for-film-editors/)). The ducking-last rule is a hard constraint of the tool: Premiere's Generate Keyframes writes volume keyframes onto the music clip and **removes any keyframes already on it**, and CrumplePop warns that changing levels after ducking means redoing it ([Adobe Learn](https://www.adobe.com/learn/premiere-pro/web/automatic-audio-ducking); [CrumplePop](https://crumplepop.com/audio-ducking-in-premiere-pro-tips-and-advices)). School of Motion's broader point is that sound added at the end is a mistake because it can drive "half OR MORE of the emotional impact," which is why temp music and the spotting pass belong before final animation, not after ([School of Motion](https://www.schoolofmotion.com/tutorials/making-giants-sound-design)). SFX Engine's suggestion to budget **30-40% of post time** for sound and mix is unsourced within its own article, but it is the only time allocation any source offers.

The Premiere track layout that supports this order is a top-down stack: dialogue on top, SFX below, music as the bed at the bottom, each group routed to a named submix (DIALOGUE, SFX, MUSIC) added via Add Tracks and routed in the Audio Track Mixer ([Frame.io](https://blog.frame.io/2021/08/16/premiere-pro-audio-mixing-basics/)). For a brand spot that means roughly A1 VO, A2 VO pickups, A3-A5 SFX (hits and transitions, foley and design, ambience bed), A6-A7 music on two tracks so cues can overlap, plus the three submixes. Submixes are not overhead even on a 30-second piece: one effect instance can process a whole group, and the stem exports in the final section hang off the submix solo buttons.

## Spotting by hero moment, and the two-and-a-half rule that governs restraint

A spotting pass is one timecoded "sound map" of every moment that needs a decision. IRPR's spotting guide covers six areas (dialogue, effects, foley, ambience, music in and out, and silence) and its one rule is "log every decision with timecode and intent" ([IRPR Sound](http://sounddesign.irpr.agency/guides/how-to-spot-a-film-for-sound/)). Krotos describes it as a single quick pass marking sync points, hits, ambience changes and tone-reference moments, with dialogue intelligibility as the top priority ([Krotos](https://krotos.studio/blog/film-sound-design)). In Premiere this becomes one marker per event named with category and intent, for example "HIT logo land," "AMB office to exterior," "SIL hold before CTA," "MX in." Silence is a category in its own right, logged beside the others, not an absence. The brief limitation is that no source publishes an actual spotting sheet for a commercial or a typical event count for a 60-second spot, so the column shape is inferred from the categories.

Which cuts get a sound is where film principles do the deciding. Walter Murch found that an audience can follow "**two and a half** thematic elements at any moment," discovered it while syncing robot footsteps, and used it on the Apocalypse Now mix after his separately mixed premixes combined at equal level "sounded like mud"; the fix was to keep only 2.5 things active at a time so "you've heard everything all the time, even though you haven't" ([NFTS](https://nfts.co.uk/article/triple-oscar-winning-sound-designer-editor-walter-murch-deconstructs-iconic-sequence)). Randy Thom's companion rules are "if everything is loud, nothing is loud," "sonic focus is the most important thing in any mix," and sometimes "the best sound design is no sound or virtually no sound at all" ([Frame.io, Randy Thom](https://blog.frame.io/2021/07/14/art-of-the-cut-audio-design-skywalker-sound-randy-thom/)). For a brand film the conversion is direct: at any second, one hero element leads (VO or music), one supports (ambience or an effect), and at most one half-weight accent sits on top. The audit question for every second of the film is whether a listener can name the one thing they are meant to hear.

The decision rule that fits every source is that a cut gets a designed sound only when something visibly moves, lands or makes contact, when the cut changes place, time or chapter, or when it is a hero moment (logo, product reveal, title, call to action). Beverly Boy restricts motion cues to visible movement ("a text swipe, a scene wipe, a camera shift, or a product sliding into view") and says the sound "should reinforce the motion rather than dominate it" ([Beverly Boy](https://beverlyboy.com/blog/transition-sound-design-that-strengthens-commercial-video-editing)). Motion The Agency lists "every movement has a sound effect" as a top-five mistake and states "good sound design knows when to stay quiet" ([Motion The Agency](https://www.motiontheagency.com/blog/sound-design-examples-before-after-in-motion)). Hemrick's advice is to "start learning to take things away rather than add things" ([Voices.com](https://www.voices.com/podcasts/voicebranding/?p=559)). Munzie Thind of GCRS London put the silence half most bluntly: "If I can get silence into a piece of work, that's half of my job done" ([Sound Design Live via Goodreads](https://www.goodreads.com/author_blog_posts/9620772-silence-the-secret-ingredient-to-sound-design-in-advertising?tab=book)). No source gives a numeric silence ratio; the practical rule is a hierarchy, with music and ambience continuous, designed SFX sparse (one per hero beat), and at least one real drop toward near-silence before the biggest moment.

The trailer and music-video crafts supply the structure for that drop. Trailer designer Bryan Jerden says cuts are "dictated by the rhythm and tempo of a score," effects must "land in time with the beat," tonal effects must not be "out of key with the music," and silence "is such a strong statement that it really needs to be well thought out" at picture edit; his "silence" is not a mute but effects mixed very low through filters and delay ([Designing Sound, Jerden](https://designingsound.org/2012/07/19/exclusive-interview-bryan-jerden-on-trailer-sound-design/)). Krotos's trailer recipe is a riser of two swells, then at the cut remove almost everything "leaving only a hit transient and a low-end drop," then hold the volume down for an aftershock of sparse drones, and it warns that power "comes from keeping dynamic range rather than overcompressing" ([Krotos trailer tips](https://www.krotosaudio.com/trailer-sound-design-tips-tricks/)). Jerden adds that sub-bass "does not have an effect on the TASA meters," which is the physics behind the recipe: low end carries weight without raising measured loudness ([Designing Sound](https://designingsound.org/2012/07/19/exclusive-interview-bryan-jerden-on-trailer-sound-design/)). Thom's "sonic refuge," a roughly 10-second moment where sound can drop, and Murch's density logic together imply that a 60-90 second piece needs one deliberate dip for the climax to read as loud ([Frame.io](https://blog.frame.io/2021/07/14/art-of-the-cut-audio-design-skywalker-sound-randy-thom/)). For pacing, sound leading picture controls felt time: FilmDaft puts a dialogue J-cut at about **0.5 seconds** and a pre-lap into a major sequence at about **2 seconds** for "real anticipation" ([FilmDaft](https://filmdaft.com/the-l-cut-and-j-cut-how-film-editors-use-audio-to-control-time/)), and Skip Lievsay describes how sounds freed from the cuts and allowed "their own natural ebb and flow" turn "more into music," which is the choice between a montage that feels continuous and one that feels choppy ([Designing Sound, Lievsay](https://designingsound.org/2013/03/music-sound-and-the-worlds-in-between-a-skip-lievsay-interview-part-1/)).

For music-video-style edits cut to a licensed track, the rules are thin but consistent: effects go in non-musical sections (intro, breakdown, pre-drop), the song's lead element is never masked, and delivery is synced to the released master ([IRPR, music videos](http://sounddesign.irpr.agency/guides/sound-design-for-music-videos-complete-guide/)). Morphic's mixing guidance is to dip the music "a few dB rather than muting it" for the length of the cue plus about half a second either side, duck the music rather than boost the effect, and pick effects that sit in a spectral gap of the track, for example a bright mid-heavy click over a bass-driven song; a tight fast-decaying thump cuts through where a slow rumble smears ([Morphic](https://morphic.com/resources/how-to/how-to-mix-sound-effects-with-music)). No named music-video sound designer with concrete levels surfaced, so these remain the best available rules.

## The palette: real materials from Soundly, three layers per hit, whoosh 10-20 ms early

Ben Burtt's standard is the right one for brand SFX: sounds should be "believable, not identifiable," real objects transformed until they lose "specific identity" while keeping a feeling the listener trusts ([StarWars.com, Burtt](https://www.starwars.com/news/empire-at-40-ben-burtt-interview)). For luxury beauty specifically, the people who design packaging sound for the houses describe a quiet, material vocabulary: "a metal cap meeting a glass shoulder, tissue paper unfolding, and a muted jewelry-case click," a "glass-on-glass chime," a "soft air rustle without friction," a "succinct spritz"; approved words are "whisper," "muted click," "chime," "crisp," "precise," "reassuring," and the rejected ones are "friction, rubbing, hissing," "whistling," "suction" and any "indefinite sound," which reads as a defective product; "in high-end beauty today, the prevailing ambition is clearly the absence of sound" ([Formes de Luxe](https://www.formesdeluxe.com/article/sound-design-reign-of-silence.65163)). Nicolas Titeux built a luxury watch commercial from recorded watch movements, metal objects and servo motors for the real part, plus electromagnetic-microphone recordings and synths for the cinematic part ([Nicolas Titeux](https://www.nicolastiteux.com/en/project/urwerk-commercial-sound-design/)). The implication for Soundly searches is to search for the actual material (cap, glass, clasp, tissue, brush, cream) rather than for "whoosh," and to treat the air and fabric pass as the only whoosh a luxury film needs. The palette assembled from these sources holds about five families: close-miked material contact, air and breath, a mono sub pulse under logo and product lands only, tonal elements that belong to the music key, and a room-tone bed so drops never read as dead air. That count is an inference; no source publishes a number of families, and no source describes the sound of a specific a client, Chanel or Dior film.

The layering recipe is the same across sources. Krotos's Premiere guide builds a hit from "three layers (transient, body, texture), with a few frames of timing shift for impact" ([Krotos, Premiere](https://krotos.studio/blog/sound-design-premiere)). Pixflow assigns each layer a frequency range (**sub 20-80 Hz** for weight, kept mono; body 80-500 Hz; character 500 Hz-4 kHz, usually real foley; transient 4-12 kHz for attack; air above 10 kHz), says a subtle UI click may need two layers and a huge explosion eight, snaps the loudest peak of every layer to the same point because "even a few milliseconds of drift can smear the impact," high-passes any layer that does not own the low end, and makes weight by duplicating a layer and pitching the copy down about an octave ([Pixflow, layering](https://pixflow.net/blog/?p=92769)). Beverly Boy's transition stack is an impact on the cut, a low rumble beneath, and a short motion cue carrying into the next shot, with the primary hit locked to the cut frame and secondary layers nudged a few frames ([Beverly Boy](https://beverlyboy.com/blog/transition-sound-design-that-strengthens-commercial-video-editing)). For whooshes, Pixflow's numbers are the only specific ones found: fast whooshes under about **0.5 s**, slow sweeps 1-3 s, start the whoosh **10-20 ms before the move begins**, align its peak energy with the fastest moment of the motion, fine-tune in one-frame steps, high-pass below 80-100 Hz, a broad 1-3 kHz lift to cut through, and never repeat the same whoosh back to back ([Pixflow, whoosh](https://pixflow.net/blog/cinematic-whoosh-sound-effects/)). Variation is cheap insurance: Krotos recommends randomizing playback speed and varying impact samples so the same hit is never reused across cuts ([Krotos trailer tips](https://www.krotosaudio.com/trailer-sound-design-tips-tricks/)).

In Premiere, three SFX tracks per designed event (TRANSIENT, BODY, SUB) is enough, with the Parametric EQ high-pass on every clip except the sub and the sub reserved for logo and product lands. Soundly feeds this directly: its Premiere panel (installed from Soundly's Settings) sends files to the timeline and syncs them to markers, the **S key** sends to the target app with handles, Alt/Opt+S to a second target, Shift+S sends flagged files, and dragging a sound out bakes any pitch, speed or reverse edit into the exported file ([Soundly news](https://getsoundly.com/news/meet-the-new-soundly-built-to-work-with-adobe-premiere/); [Soundly FAQ, shortcuts](https://getsoundly.com/faq/what-shortcuts-are-available-in-soundly/index.html.md); [getsoundly.com](https://getsoundly.com)). Two housekeeping facts matter for a one-person setup: Soundly downloads cloud files to the folder set under Settings > Download, so point it at the project's audio folder per project before spotting, and dragging into Premiere brings only the selected range ([ProVideo Coalition](https://www.provideocoalition.com/soundly-2-sound-effects-platform/)). The Pro library is 250,000 sounds at 24-bit/96 kHz minimum, the metadata panel supports UCS 8.2.1, and Freesound is searchable in-app ([getsoundly.com](https://getsoundly.com)). Soundly's handle length and exact marker-sync steps for Premiere are not documented in any fetched page, which is the one practical gap in the integration.

## Mixing VO, music and SFX in Premiere: the voice is the anchor, music 12-18 dB under it

There is no single industry number for the voice-to-music offset, and the spread is instructive. Broadcast practice puts music **8-16 dB under dialogue** ([Desktop Documentaries](https://www.desktop-documentaries.com/audio-levels-for-video-and-documentary-production.html)), while 2026 online-video practice puts it **18-25 dB under**, simplified to "about 20 dB," with speech normalized to about -14 LUFS ([Zella](https://zellahq.com/blog/music-ducking-explained/)). The gap reflects playback: a TV with a center speaker tolerates louder music, a phone speaker does not. Krotos's rule of thumb on a clip meter is music starting around **-20 dB**, footsteps and environmental SFX near -20 dB, and hits and loud sounds up to about **-10 dB**, with dialogue taking "most of the mix" ([Krotos, balance](https://krotos.studio/blog/how-to-balance-music-and-sound-effects)). Frame.io's Premiere guide puts dialogue and VO at -15 to -12 dB, underscore at -22 to -18 dB, and the mix peaking below -6 dB ([Frame.io](https://blog.frame.io/2021/08/16/premiere-pro-audio-mixing-basics/)). Motion The Agency's non-negotiable is that "voiceover should always be the clearest element in the audio" ([Motion The Agency](https://www.motiontheagency.com/blog/sound-design-examples-before-after-in-motion)), and Joseph Nilo's is to never lower the dialogue to make room for loud music ([Joseph Nilo](https://josephnilo.com/blog/dialogue-loudness-lufs-true-peak-video/)). The working set for a phone-first brand film is music 12-18 dB under the VO during speech, swelling 6-10 dB in real gaps, SFX hits peaking up to 6-10 dB above the music bed, never above the VO as the loudest sustained element.

Premiere's ducking is the first pass, not the finish. The documented path is: tag VO clips as Dialogue and music as Music in Essential Sound, run Loudness > Auto-Match on both, enable Ducking on the music, set Sensitivity (Adobe's own example had to lower it because "nearly the whole clip ducked"), Reduce By, and Fades in milliseconds (Adobe's example is 800 ms), then Generate Keyframes ([Adobe Learn](https://www.adobe.com/learn/premiere-pro/web/automatic-audio-ducking)). Practitioner values are **-12 dB Reduce By for subtle, -18 dB for noticeable**, and Premiere 2025 adds Fade Position, where Outside starts the dip before the first word and Inside keeps it within the dialogue ([Magnific](https://www.magnific.com/blog/how-to-use-auto-ducking-in-premiere-pro-for-clean-professional-audio/)). Adobe's Audition engineers explain the Sensitivity behavior: mid-range values trigger the most adjustments and produce an "FM-Radio" pumping effect between pauses, and faster fades suit fast music with fast speech while slower fades suit background music under VO ([Adobe Blog](https://blog.adobe.com/en/publish/2017/11/02/audition-deep-dive-auto-ducking-music)). Zella's timing guidance is an attack under about **300 ms**, a release slower than the attack, swells only on pauses of 2 s or more, and an outro fade over the last 1-2 s ([Zella](https://zellahq.com/blog/music-ducking-explained/)). The failure modes to expect are uniform reduction across a clip, ducking at both ends of a clip, and regeneration wiping manual keyframes, so the hybrid CrumplePop recommends is panel first, hand-edit last ([CrumplePop](https://crumplepop.com/audio-ducking-in-premiere-pro-tips-and-advices)). Duck against Dialogue only, not SFX, or every hit pumps the music. No fetched source confirms Premiere's factory defaults for the three sliders, and sidechain compression is not part of the native ducking path.

The VO chain and the EQ carve are where the remaining clarity comes from, and the order of fixes is level first, EQ last. Ty Ford's 2005 advice still holds as the consensus: turn the music down before carving, and when carving, "cut around 2k-4k" in the music where speech sits, not so drastically that the music is damaged ([DV Info Net](https://www.dvinfo.net/forum/archive/index.php/t-41476.html)). Sage Audio's voice chain runs gate, EQ, de-esser, compressor, saturation, limiter: attenuate the lows, small cuts near 300 and 700 Hz, a boost at **1.5-2.5 kHz** for clarity, a split-band de-esser taking **3-4 dB**, a compressor at **3:1 soft knee for 3-4 dB of gain reduction** with a release near 100 ms ([Sage Audio](https://www.sageaudio.com/articles/how-to-make-voice-over-sound-better)). Sound On Sound's warning is that compression alone never guarantees a consistent voice level and that fader automation does ([Sound On Sound](https://www.soundonsound.com/sound-advice/10-essential-mix-checks)). For phone playback, Mr. Vocal adds a small dip around 200 Hz where phone speakers build mud and about 1.5 dB of soft clipping before the limiter to keep transients through transcodes ([Mr. Vocal](https://mrvocal.com/posts/loudness-for-shorts)). In Premiere terms that is Essential Sound Dialogue (Repair, Clarity) or Parametric EQ for the cuts and lift, the DeEsser effect, a compressor at 3:1, and the Hard Limiter at the end of the VO chain, with a wide 2-4 dB bell cut around 2.5-3.5 kHz on the music. No 2023-2026 source states a VO high-pass frequency; the 80-100 Hz figure comes from the SFX-side sources and is common practice ([SFX Engine](https://sfxengine.com/blog/common-sound-design-mistakes-in-video-editing)).

One scripting fact matters for anyone automating this through the Premiere MCP: the supported ExtendScript API can place clips, mute tracks, disable clips and write Volume keyframes on any keyframeable parameter, but Adobe's Bruce Bullis confirmed "there's no supported way to apply effects using PPro's ExtendScript API," and the QE DOM workaround has been breaking since Premiere 14.3 ([Adobe Community, 2019](https://community.adobe.com/t5/premiere-pro-discussions/add-audio-effect-using-script/m-p/10559331); [Adobe Community, 2020](https://community.adobe.com/t5/premiere-pro-discussions/extendscript-no-longer-able-to-add-effects-using-qe-dom/m-p/12795445/highlight/true)). Placement, fades and ducking rides are scriptable; EQ, compression and Essential Sound tags are hand work.

## Delivery: -14 LUFS and -1 dBTP for web, R128 or A/85 for TV, stems unlimited

The loudness targets fall into two families, and the key behavior is that platforms turn loud content down and never turn quiet content up, so overshooting buys nothing. YouTube normalizes to about **-14 LUFS integrated** and "will not increase the loudness of your video," so the rule of thumb is -14 LUFS with a **-1 dBTP** ceiling ([Youlean](https://youlean.co/?p=30813)). Instagram, Facebook and TikTok publish no target; the "-14 internal" figure is an estimate, with -1 dBTP advised ([Fora Soft](https://www.forasoft.com/learn/audio-for-video/articles-audio/lufs-targets-per-platform-2026)). For dialogue-led corporate work with no spec supplied, Nilo's default is **-16 LUFS** (range -14 to -18), with -2 dBTP "with aggressive encoding, multiple generations, or sharp transients," and he notes LRA is "not meaningful for programmes under one minute" ([Joseph Nilo](https://josephnilo.com/blog/dialogue-loudness-lufs-true-peak-video/)). Broadcast is law, not preference: **EBU R128 -23 LUFS, -1 dBTP** in Europe, and **ATSC A/85 -24 LKFS, -2 dBTP**, mandatory for US TV ads under the CALM Act, with A/85 the default for commercials and interstitials ([Fora Soft](https://www.forasoft.com/learn/audio-for-video/articles-audio/lufs-targets-per-platform-2026); [TC Electronic](https://www.tcelectronic.com/broadcast-standards)). Netflix sits at -27 LKFS dialogue-gated, -2 dBTP ([Joseph Nilo](https://josephnilo.com/blog/dialogue-loudness-lufs-true-peak-video/)). One practitioner reports that in 2026 TikTok, Reels and Shorts turn hot masters down more aggressively and that a -8 LUFS master ends up quieter on TikTok than a -14 one; this is a single source, not platform documentation ([Mr. Vocal](https://mrvocal.com/posts/loudness-for-shorts)). No fetched source covers event LED-wall or PA playback, so the only guidance there is inference from physics: large reverberant rooms smear dense music under dialogue, so lean toward 15 dB or more of music offset and restrained hits, since PA limiters will squash them.

The measuring and export path in Premiere is concrete. The Loudness Meter lives on the Mix track in the Audio Track Mixer (Special > Loudness Meter), carries presets for EBU, ATSC, Spotify, Apple Podcasts, Netflix and YouTube, reads Momentary (400 ms), Short-Term (3 s) and Integrated, and does not change levels ([Adobe HelpX, Loudness Meter](https://helpx.adobe.com/pl/premiere-pro/using/loudness-radar.html)). Nilo's order is spec, editorial audio, meter after the limiter, measure the whole programme, correct, measure again, export, then re-measure the encoded file for loudness, true peak, channels and sync ([Joseph Nilo](https://josephnilo.com/blog/dialogue-loudness-lufs-true-peak-video/)). For a broadcast version, the export Loudness Normalization effect in Premiere and Media Encoder applies ATSC A/85, EBU R128 or ITU BS.1770-3 with a true-peak limiter and can write an XML loudness report ([Adobe Help, loudness correction](https://helpx.adobe.com/media-encoder/desktop/encoding-and-exporting/automatic-loudness-correction.html)); Larry Jordan sets its true-peak limiter Release to 200 ms to reduce audible pumping and calls it "usually unnecessary, though harmless" for web ([Larry Jordan](https://larryjordan.com/articles/adobe-media-encoder-loudness-normalization/)). Adobe's recommended delivery audio is **AAC, 48 kHz, 320 kbps stereo** ([Adobe](https://www.adobe.com/creativecloud/video/hub/guides/best-export-settings-for-premiere-pro)).

Stems are standard delivery so dialogue can be reused without music, music re-leveled later or languages swapped, even when the editor mixed it ([Mixing Light](https://mixinglight.com/color-grading-tutorials/how-to-export-multi-channel-audio-stems-adobe-premiere-pro); [Jonny Elwyn](https://jonnyelwyn.co.uk/film-and-video-editing/sound-design-tips-for-film-editors/)). Premiere still has no one-click per-track export in 25.x; the options are three audio-only WAV exports (48 kHz, 24-bit) with each submix soloed in turn, or a multichannel sequence with tracks routed to discrete output pairs exported as QuickTime with Discrete channel layout ([PremiumBeat](https://www.premiumbeat.com/blog/export-multiple-audio-tracks-premiere-pro); [Adobe Community, 2025](https://community.adobe.com/t5/premiere-pro-bugs/why-does-premiere-pro-make-it-so-hard-to-export-multi-track-audio/m-p/15367470)). Krotos's naming example is "Scene05_Dialogue_v02_48kHz24bit.wav" and its stem set is Dialogue, Music, SFX and Full mix ([Krotos, Premiere](https://krotos.studio/blog/sound-design-premiere)). Sound On Sound's warning applies directly: stems must sum to the stereo mix, which is "harder if you use bus dynamics processing," so the Hard Limiter sits on the master only for the mix export and stems go out unlimited with 2-3 dB of headroom ([Sound On Sound](https://www.soundonsound.com/sound-advice/10-essential-mix-checks)).

The checks are cheap and the sources are unanimous about them. Check in mono, because single wireless speakers sum both channels and phase-widened elements can "sound thin or disappear" ([Production Expert](https://www.production-expert.com/production-expert-1/2018/6/21/your-audio-mixes-are-at-risk-of-sounding-bad-on-modern-gear-if-you-dont-start-doing-this-simple-test-now)). Listen on a phone, earbuds and a laptop, since Pro Sound Effects estimates "99.9% of listeners" have cheap speakers, and judge SFX inside the full mix, never soloed ([Pro Sound Effects](https://blog.prosoundeffects.com/sound-design-mistakes-you-should-never-make); [Frame.io](https://blog.frame.io/2021/08/16/premiere-pro-audio-mixing-basics/)). Screen-record real platform playback and match a known-good reference within 1 dB; if the platform version plays 3 dB quieter than the reference, the master was too hot ([Mr. Vocal](https://mrvocal.com/posts/loudness-for-shorts)). Krotos cites TENET as the famous case of music and SFX too loud and dialogue too quiet on home systems ([Krotos, balance](https://krotos.studio/blog/how-to-balance-music-and-sound-effects)).

The amateur tells gather into one list, each paired with its fix. Krotos names "an obvious one-shot whoosh, reverb that doesn't match the room, ambience borrowed from another scene, and repeated identical sounds," plus "relying on single stock hits instead of layers" ([Krotos, Premiere](https://krotos.studio/blog/sound-design-premiere)). Pro Sound Effects adds untrimmed tops and tails, too many layers, skipping the cheap-speaker check, designing in a vacuum, forgetting the high-pass filter (bass build-up) and "reverbing to death" ([Pro Sound Effects](https://blog.prosoundeffects.com/sound-design-mistakes-you-should-never-make)). Motion The Agency's five are music overpowering VO, key interactions with no sound, music that does not match brand tone, a sound on every movement, and abrupt audio cuts ([Motion The Agency](https://www.motiontheagency.com/blog/sound-design-examples-before-after-in-motion)). Beverly Boy adds random rather than frame-locked placement and "aggressive sounds for a clean technology brand" ([Beverly Boy](https://beverlyboy.com/blog/transition-sound-design-that-strengthens-commercial-video-editing)). The sum of these is the "cheap corporate" signature: one library whoosh on every cut, a braam under the logo, music at a constant level under the VO, no ambience bed, no silence. The premium signature is the exact inverse: few events, each built from two or three layers of real material, a continuous bed, a real drop before the hero moment, and music ducked under the voice. No source frames AI-generated SFX as a tell; the only mention of AI is a vendor promoting its own generator.

## Conclusion

The method is less a set of techniques than a single discipline applied at every stage: decide what the listener should hear at each second, and remove or duck everything that competes with it. That is what Murch's 2.5 rule, Thom's sonic focus, Jerden's "everything has to work with the music," and the ad designer's "silence is half my job" all describe from different seats, and it is why the one-person workflow works at all. A solo editor cannot out-layer a post house, but the sources show the post house is not winning by layering; it wins by choosing, and choosing costs nothing but nerve. The practical consequence is that the spotting pass, not the mix, is where a brand film's sound quality is decided, and that a marker list of twelve hero moments built from real materials will beat sixty library whooshes every time.

The remaining uncertainty is all numeric, and it should be treated as such. The ducking amounts, the voice-to-music offsets and the social-platform loudness figures are practitioner convergence rather than published specs, and the two trustworthy constants are the broadcast laws (-23 LUFS R128, -24 LKFS A/85) and the fact that web platforms only turn audio down. The right response is to lock the process (VO anchor, bed, layered events, duck last, meter after the limiter, re-measure the encoded file, stems unlimited) and let the few numbers that vary by platform be checked against a screen recording of real playback, which is the one test that no spec can replace.
